Hospital Autonomous Secure Delivery Car

An autonomous medical delivery robot that drives a corridor, stops for obstacles, and only opens to an authorized RFID tag. The electronics were committed to a custom single layer shield instead of left on a breadboard.

PCB design, power system, integration and test

The problem

Moving supplies between hospital departments is repetitive, time sensitive, and carries a chain of custody requirement. The brief was a small vehicle that drives itself down a corridor, refuses to run into anything, and will not release its cargo to the wrong person.

The shield

Getting the build off the breadboard.

The electronics were committed to a single layer shield designed in Altium Designer that stacks straight onto an Arduino Uno. Every subsystem gets its own labelled header: the 5 V power sensor, the ultrasonic sensor, the RFID reader, the LCD, the Bluetooth module, the servo and the motor driver, plus an onboard push button with its 10 K pull down.

Silkscreening every net meant the harness could only be assembled one way, which removed an entire class of integration bug from the project.

Two rails, one battery

Motor noise was the failure mode, so the power system was designed around it.

  • One 11.1 V LiPo feeds two buck converters, producing a 5.1 V logic rail and a 7.9 V motor rail.
  • Separating them keeps the commutation noise of the drive motors off the sensor and I2C bus.
  • That separation is what made the ultrasonic readings stable enough to trust as a safety input rather than a suggestion.

Sensing and actuation

Five sensors and four actuator systems on one controller.

  • HC-SR04 ultrasonic for obstacle range, stopping the vehicle inside a 20 cm threshold.
  • RC522 RFID for access control, so the cargo compartment only opens to an authorized tag.
  • HC-05 Bluetooth for commands and telemetry off the vehicle.
  • DHT11 for cabin temperature and humidity, which matters for anything temperature sensitive in transit.
  • A 16 by 2 LCD for local status, two DC drive motors through an L298N, and a servo on the latch.

Validation

Measured

45 minutes of continuous runtime on one charge, 100 percent of integration tests passed, and the obstacle stop verified below the 20 cm threshold.
